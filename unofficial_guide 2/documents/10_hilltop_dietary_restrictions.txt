Source: The Hilltop (Howard University student newspaper) — Investigative
Title: "Students with Dietary Restrictions Face Limitations in On-Campus Dining Spaces"
By Kori McNair, published ~4 months ago

"I stopped eating at the cafeteria because it just wasn't safe," Maya Rose said. Rose attended Howard for her freshman year but said she had to leave due to a lack of food allergy accommodations. She is allergic to dairy, soy, and has an anaphylactic allergy to all nuts, along with Celiac disease and another deficiency restricting carbs and sugars.

Each day, Rose reached out to the chef-on-duty to see which foods were safe to eat. French fries and salads were the primary foods she could eat, and even those had cross-contamination issues. A cabinet with gluten-free options in the dining halls was frequently empty by the time she got to it — "it was free for all and not safe for the people actually allergic," Rose said.

Rose eventually opted out of her meal plan and cooked in her dorm instead, which made it harder to socialize with friends during meals. Before her sophomore year, after providing medical documentation requesting a dorm with a kitchen and minimal roommates, she was placed with five roommates in Howard Plaza Towers instead — Residence Life said her doctor's note didn't specifically state that accommodation. She transferred to the University of Arizona in Tucson rather than return.

Last summer, Howard switched dining services from Sodexo to Aramark. Assistant VP of Auxiliary Services LaNiece Tyree said in an Aramark press release she was excited to "elevate and innovate" the dining experience. Some students say the limited options for those with food allergies have remained the same regardless of the switch.

Henna Lopez-Spears, a sophomore international affairs major, is pescatarian with allergies to dairy, gluten, sesame, soy and egg. She said the vegan station wasn't well advertised — many students didn't know dairy-free milk, gluten-free bread, and dairy-free cheese were available on request.

"Not a lot of people knew that they had to ask for it, because we didn't even know that they were there," Lopez-Spears said.

Executive Director of Auxiliary Enterprises LeVita Basset said the university offers dedicated vegan/vegetarian stations (PlantForward in Bethune Annex, Root in Blackburn) that are "routinely monitored," but acknowledged "menu changes and human error can occur, which is why we are continuously improving our verification processes."

First- and second-year students living on-campus are required to have a meal plan; there are four residential and two commuter dining plans. Lopez-Spears said the vegan station in Annex "isn't fully vegan" — only the rice was reliably vegan, and she had to specifically ask for vegan meat, which staff then had to retrieve from the kitchen.

The university said it is developing a "comprehensive dining guide" and hosts biweekly food advisory committee meetings, plus a "Voice of the Consumer" feedback platform.

Cross-contamination:
Howard's dining halls have allergen-avoidance stations — "True Balance" in Bethune Annex (AllerCheck certified, meaning the service follows procedures to minimize cross-contact, though it does not guarantee allergen-free spaces) and the "Stress Less Zone" in Blackburn Café (peanut/tree-nut/gluten-free options). Basset said staff are trained to activate emergency response procedures, including contacting emergency services and campus police, if a reaction occurs.

Kaaria Lopez-Spears (Henna's sister), a freshman with severe allergies to dairy, soy, eggs, gluten, all nuts, oranges and pineapples, said she avoids areas that typically serve omelets, pasta with cheese, or pizza, even without having had a specific cross-contamination incident.

Violet Spencer, a sophomore with multiple allergies including shellfish, soy, peppers, broccoli, green beans, spinach and all nuts, described her dining experience as "bumpy." She was told during freshman housing orientation that food accommodations weren't arranged until a student received a room number. She met with a dietitian and a specific Blackburn chef who helped prepare her meals daily — but this broke down on weekends when Blackburn was closed and she wasn't initially told Annex had options for her.

"I felt very cared for by them, but it was just a little bit difficult when we had days off — Blackburn is closed on weekends, and I was told my freshman year that there weren't really a lot of options in Annex," Spencer said. She has since switched to eating primarily at Annex and has a group chat directly with Annex's chefs and workers to coordinate meals.

Basset said the university's process for dietary accommodations involves an initial consultation with a registered dietitian, submission of medical documentation, and collaboration between dining services and student accessibility to determine accommodations.

Some students with allergies or restrictions have turned to off-campus grocery shopping or restaurants instead. Destiny Givens, a sophomore with a condition triggered by dairy, processed foods, fried foods, and high-sodium foods, said she now spends around $300/month on groceries — up from her freshman year when more accommodating on-campus options were available before the Sodexo-to-Aramark switch.
